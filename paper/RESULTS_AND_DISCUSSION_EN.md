# 3. Results and discussion

## 3.1 Overall performance across forecasting horizons

Table 1 compares the overall performance of STaR-GNN with recurrent, multi-scale, and graph-based forecasting models for the 24 h and 168 h horizons. Figure 1 complements the absolute values in Table 1 by showing the relative reductions in MAE, MAPE, and RMSE and the absolute improvement in NSE, defined as $\Delta\mathrm{NSE}=\mathrm{NSE}_{\mathrm{STaR-GNN}}-\mathrm{NSE}_{\mathrm{baseline}}$. Positive values in both panels therefore favor STaR-GNN.

STaR-GNN achieved the best value of all four metrics at both horizons. For the 24 h task, its MAE, MAPE, RMSE, and NSE were 9.424, 1.805%, 5.535, and 0.981, respectively. Relative to GRU, LSTM, MSNet, and the three MSCMNet variants, STaR-GNN reduced MAE by 34.9–46.7%, MAPE by 30.6–43.6%, and RMSE by 27.0–45.7%, while increasing NSE by 0.022–0.065. Even against MSCMNet-W, the strongest of these models overall, the reductions were 34.9%, 30.6%, and 27.0%, respectively, and NSE increased from 0.959 to 0.981. The improvement thus extended across measures of absolute error, relative error, large-error sensitivity, and goodness of fit rather than being confined to one evaluation criterion.

The advantage was also evident in the like-for-like comparisons with the graph-based baselines. Relative to DCRNN, STaR-GNN reduced the 24 h MAE, MAPE, and RMSE by 20.9%, 18.5%, and 19.2% and increased NSE by 0.010. The corresponding changes relative to STGCN were 23.7%, 25.6%, and 30.0%, with a 0.020 increase in NSE. Because all three graph models exploit cross-DMA dependence, these comparisons indicate that the performance gain cannot be attributed to graph representation alone. They are instead consistent with an additional benefit from explicitly modelling changes in demand state and forecast-position-dependent daily patterns.

Extending the horizon to 168 h increased errors or reduced NSE for most models, but STaR-GNN retained the best overall performance, with an MAE of 12.234, MAPE of 2.014%, RMSE of 6.161, and NSE of 0.976. Compared with the six recurrent or multi-scale models, STaR-GNN reduced MAE by 18.2–34.5%, MAPE by 22.5–37.1%, and RMSE by 20.6–45.7%, while increasing NSE by 0.016–0.058. In the common 46-origin evaluation, the reductions relative to DCRNN were 27.2%, 38.0%, and 37.2%, with a 0.037 increase in NSE; those relative to STGCN were 16.0%, 43.7%, and 40.2%, with a 0.043 increase in NSE. The larger week-ahead gains in MAPE, RMSE, and NSE suggest that the advantage of STaR-GNN becomes more consequential as forecast errors accumulate. The evidence nevertheless supports the specific conclusion that STaR-GNN outperformed every comparator; it does not support the broader claim that graph-based models invariably outperform sequence models, because DCRNN and STGCN did not surpass MSCMNet-W on every 168 h metric.

## 3.2 Component contributions and lead-time dependence

The factorial ablation in Table 2 separates the contributions of SAS-Norm and FA-DPR, while Fig. 2 tracks their day-wise effects throughout the 168 h horizon for all four metrics. The curves and confidence intervals are horizontally offset within each forecast day to distinguish the closely spaced estimates of DCRNN + SAS-Norm and the complete STaR-GNN without altering their temporal positions.

For 24 h forecasting, adding SAS-Norm to DCRNN reduced MAE, MAPE, and RMSE by 12.2%, 9.1%, and 10.4%, respectively. Adding FA-DPR alone reduced the same metrics by 5.7%, 12.1%, and 11.2%. The complete model produced larger reductions of 20.9%, 18.5%, and 19.2% and the highest NSE. Both components therefore contributed at the day-ahead horizon, and their effects were not fully redundant.

At 168 h, SAS-Norm accounted for most of the improvement in overall error magnitude. It reduced MAE, MAPE, and RMSE relative to DCRNN by 27.3%, 35.3%, and 34.1% and increased NSE from 0.940 to 0.974. FA-DPR alone reduced MAE by 16.2% and RMSE by 4.9% and increased NSE by 0.006, but its MAPE increased slightly from 3.248% to 3.278%. Its isolated contribution was therefore metric-dependent rather than uniformly beneficial. The complete STaR-GNN obtained the best 168 h MAPE, RMSE, and NSE (2.014%, 6.161, and 0.976, respectively).

The 168 h MAE requires a more qualified interpretation. DCRNN + SAS-Norm obtained 12.208, compared with 12.234 for STaR-GNN, a difference of only 0.026 (0.21% of the SAS-Norm value). The 95% moving-block confidence interval for the paired mean difference between the complete model and SAS-Norm was −0.129 to 0.177 and included zero. The two configurations should thus be regarded as statistically indistinguishable on this metric, whereas the complete model provided the more balanced result across MAPE, RMSE, and NSE.

The lead-time analysis further clarifies how these contributions developed over the forecast week. The MAE reduction of STaR-GNN relative to DCRNN increased from 13.4% on day 1 (95% CI: 10.6–16.1%) to 34.0% on day 7 (30.3–38.0%). It improved 43 of the 46 forecast origins on day 1 and all 46 on day 7. SAS-Norm exhibited a similar increase, from 14.2% to 33.9%, confirming its central role in limiting week-ahead error growth. The MAE reduction from FA-DPR increased from 2.7% on day 1 to 18.5% on day 7, but its MAPE and RMSE deteriorated on some intermediate and late forecast days and their confidence intervals were wider. Taken together, the ablation supports complementary but unequal roles: SAS-Norm primarily stabilizes forecasts against shifts in demand level and scale, whereas FA-DPR provides forecast-position-specific information that improves selected long-lead outcomes. The present evidence does not justify claiming that FA-DPR alone improves every metric throughout the week.

## 3.3 Temporal and spatial robustness

Overall averages can conceal dependence on a small number of favorable forecast dates or DMAs. Figure 3 therefore summarizes paired comparisons across all 46 common forecast origins (Fig. 3a) and across the 10 DMAs (Fig. 3b). Color represents the proportion of comparisons improved by STaR-GNN; each cell reports the direction-aligned mean improvement and the number of wins over the number of comparisons. Supplementary Fig. S2 provides the corresponding empirical distributions of origin-level total MAE, while Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Fig. S1 report the absolute DMA-level metrics and their relative changes.

For the 24 h task, STaR-GNN reduced origin-level MAE relative to DCRNN and STGCN by an average of 19.3% and 22.6%, respectively, and improved 45 of the 46 origins in both comparisons. The corresponding 95% confidence intervals were 16.7–21.6% and 20.6–25.0%. STaR-GNN also improved MAPE, RMSE, and NSE for most, but not all, forecast origins. The day-ahead advantage was therefore temporally consistent without being universal at every date and metric.

Temporal consistency strengthened at the week-ahead horizon, particularly relative to DCRNN. STaR-GNN reduced MAE at all 46 origins by an average of 26.7% (95% CI: 24.1–29.2%) and improved MAPE, RMSE, and NSE at 45 origins. Against STGCN, MAE decreased at 40 of the 46 origins by an average of 15.1% (9.8–19.8%). Mean improvements in MAPE, RMSE, and NSE remained positive, although the respective win counts were 36, 35, and 35 of 46. This distributional evidence shows that the week-ahead advantage was not created by a few exceptionally favorable cases, while also identifying test periods in which STGCN remained competitive.

The spatial results were even more consistent. Across 10 DMAs, two horizons, two graph baselines, and four metrics, STaR-GNN improved 158 of the 160 comparisons. All 40 DMA–horizon–baseline combinations showed lower MAE. The only two exceptions occurred for DMA G at 168 h against STGCN: RMSE was 1.469 for STaR-GNN and 1.451 for STGCN, and NSE was 0.878 and 0.881, respectively. Although small, these differences preclude a claim of absolute dominance for every DMA and metric. Improvement magnitude also varied substantially among DMAs, with MAE reductions exceeding 40% in some zones but remaining below 5% in others. System-level performance should therefore be accompanied by DMA-level diagnostics when the model is used operationally.

Together, Fig. 3 and the supplementary analyses establish a progression from average accuracy to temporal, spatial, and distributional robustness. The gains were broadly shared across forecast origins and DMAs rather than being driven mainly by isolated dates or zones, but the retained exceptions reveal where additional local adaptation may be needed.

## 3.4 Week-ahead demand dynamics

Aggregate metrics do not show when errors occur within the weekly demand trajectory. Figure 4 therefore links population-level error structure to a representative 168 h forecast. Figure 4a reports the mean absolute error in aggregate demand at each hour of the daily cycle after pooling all seven forecast days and 46 origins, with moving-block 95% confidence intervals. Figure 4b shows a representative forecast selected by a pre-specified median-error rule, and Fig. 4c locates the corresponding hourly absolute errors.

Averaged over the 24 within-day positions, the absolute error of STaR-GNN was 4.920 L s$^{-1}$, compared with 7.735 L s$^{-1}$ for DCRNN and 8.574 L s$^{-1}$ for STGCN, equivalent to reductions of 36.4% and 42.6%. STaR-GNN had the lowest mean error at 22 of the 24 hourly positions relative to DCRNN and at 23 positions relative to STGCN. Its advantage therefore covered most of the diurnal cycle instead of being concentrated in low-demand periods or a few hours. The one or two remaining positions also show why this result should not be interpreted as strict superiority at every hour.

The representative case was not selected for visual attractiveness. Among the 46 common origins, origin 70 had the STaR-GNN total MAE closest to the median: 12.182 versus a median of 12.193. At the same origin, total MAE was 15.517 for DCRNN and 14.653 for STGCN. All three models reproduced the principal daily periodicity (Fig. 4b), but STaR-GNN generally followed transitions in demand level and several local peaks and troughs more closely. The hourly errors in Fig. 4c nevertheless retain several spikes during rapid changes, indicating that short-lived transitions remain difficult to predict.

This example is intended to interpret, rather than replace, the full-period evaluation. The pooled diurnal analysis provides the population-level evidence; the median-error forecast illustrates how the improvement appears in a typical weekly trajectory; and the hourly-error panel identifies residual weaknesses that are averaged out by summary metrics.

## 3.5 Integrated discussion and implications

The experiments provide a coherent sequence of evidence. The overall comparison establishes that STaR-GNN improves both day-ahead and week-ahead forecasts across four complementary metrics. The factorial ablation then attributes much of the long-horizon stabilization to SAS-Norm and identifies a smaller, metric- and lead-time-dependent contribution from FA-DPR. Origin- and DMA-level analyses show that these gains are broadly distributed in time and space, and the week-ahead trajectory analysis connects the statistical gains to the diurnal structure of water demand. This convergence is more informative than any individual mean score because it tests accuracy, component contribution, robustness, and process-level behavior separately.

Previous work has shown that graph recurrent models can exploit inter-zone relationships in multi-DMA demand forecasting (Zanfei et al., 2022), while multi-scale architectures can improve forecasts by representing temporal patterns at different resolutions (Que et al., 2024). The present results are consistent with both observations but refine them in two respects. First, the comparison with DCRNN and STGCN shows that cross-DMA graph learning alone does not account for the observed gains. Second, the ablation and lead-time results suggest that explicitly separating changing demand state from within-day shape, then retrieving historical daily patterns according to forecast position, is particularly useful when the prediction window extends across several daily cycles. This is an evidence-based interpretation of the observed behavior, not a direct identification of the physical causes of demand variation.

The stronger long-horizon performance is relevant to urban water management. Day-ahead forecasts may inform short-term production and pumping schedules, whereas week-ahead forecasts may provide earlier information for storage planning, maintenance coordination, and resource allocation. The consistent reductions across most DMAs and hours indicate potential value for coordinated multi-zone forecasting. However, no downstream control or economic optimization was evaluated here; operational benefits should therefore be expressed as potential rather than demonstrated savings. The heterogeneity among DMAs and the exceptions in DMA G further indicate that deployment should retain zone-specific monitoring rather than rely solely on aggregate demand accuracy.

Four limitations define the scope of the findings. First, the evaluation concerns one real multi-DMA system, and transferability across cities, climates, and demand regimes remains to be tested. Second, the graph represents functional dependence inferred from historical demand rather than a complete hydraulic topology and is static across operating conditions. A dynamic graph or a hybrid functional–hydraulic representation may better capture seasonal and operational changes. Third, the recurrent and MSCMNet values in the broad comparison were taken from Que et al. (2024), whereas DCRNN, STGCN, and STaR-GNN were evaluated under the common 46-origin protocol. That comparison establishes performance relative to published benchmarks, while the component, temporal, and spatial conclusions rely on direct comparisons under the common protocol. Finally, the present study evaluates deterministic point forecasts; predictive intervals and reliability under unusual demand events remain open questions.

Overall, STaR-GNN improved not only mean forecasting accuracy but also its consistency across horizons, forecast origins, DMAs, and the diurnal cycle. The evidence indicates that explicit modelling of day-scale state changes and forecast-aligned daily patterns, combined with graph-based cross-DMA learning, is the principal reason that accuracy degrades more slowly over the week-ahead horizon.
