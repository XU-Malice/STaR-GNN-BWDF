# 3. Results and discussion

## 3.1 Overall performance across forecasting horizons

To determine whether the gains of STaR-GNN extend across modelling paradigms, eight representative deep-learning baselines were considered. GRU, LSTM, MSNet, MSCMNet-WM, MSCMNet-M, and MSCMNet-W represent recurrent and multi-scale sequence forecasting, whereas DCRNN and STGCN represent spatiotemporal graph models that explicitly encode inter-DMA dependence. This design evaluates both the overall competitiveness of STaR-GNN against non-graph sequence models and whether its improvement extends beyond existing graph-learning architectures, rather than arising solely from the introduction of a graph representation.

Table 1 reports the system-level absolute metrics for the 24 h and 168 h tasks. STaR-GNN was the only method ranked first in MAE, MAPE, RMSE, and NSE at both horizons: the four values were 9.424, 1.805%, 5.535, and 0.981 at 24 h and 12.234, 2.014%, 6.161, and 0.976 at 168 h. MSCMNet-W, the strongest sequence model overall, did not surpass STaR-GNN on any system-level metric, and neither did DCRNN or STGCN at either horizon. The latter comparison is particularly informative because it indicates that the gain cannot be explained by cross-DMA graph representation alone; it is instead consistent with the explicit modelling of demand-state changes and forecast-position-dependent daily patterns in STaR-GNN.

Figure 1 quantifies the magnitude of these gains. Error metrics are expressed as relative reductions, whereas $\Delta\mathrm{NSE}=\mathrm{NSE}_{\mathrm{STaR-GNN}}-\mathrm{NSE}_{\mathrm{baseline}}$ denotes the absolute NSE improvement; positive values therefore favor STaR-GNN throughout. Against the six sequence models, STaR-GNN reduced MAE, MAPE, and RMSE by 34.9–46.7%, 30.6–43.6%, and 27.0–45.7%, respectively, at 24 h, while increasing NSE by 0.022–0.065. The corresponding improvements at 168 h were 18.2–34.5%, 22.5–37.1%, 20.6–45.7%, and 0.016–0.058. Against the two graph baselines, the three error reductions ranged from 18.5% to 30.0% at 24 h and from 16.0% to 43.7% at 168 h, with NSE gains of 0.010–0.020 and 0.037–0.043, respectively. The lead was therefore not confined to one metric or one horizon. In particular, the substantial week-ahead improvements in MAPE, RMSE, and NSE show that STaR-GNN continued to limit the deterioration in overall accuracy as the prediction window increased.

## 3.2 Cross-DMA consistency and horizon-dependent heterogeneity

The system-level comparison does not establish whether an improvement is shared across demand zones or dominated by a few large DMAs. Figure 2 therefore evaluates the distribution of signed DMA-level improvements against each baseline separately. This effect-size representation was preferred to within-DMA ranks because it preserves both the direction and magnitude of each comparison. Across the eight baselines and four metrics, STaR-GNN yielded positive improvements in 306 of 320 pairwise DMA comparisons (95.6%) at 24 h and 275 of 320 (85.9%) at 168 h. The pattern also extended beyond the sequence models: all 80 comparisons with DCRNN and STGCN were positive at 24 h, and 78 of 80 remained positive at 168 h. The distributions in Fig. 2 thus indicate broad cross-DMA consistency, while their negative tails make clear that the advantage was not spatially uniform.

Figure 3 applies a more stringent local criterion by comparing STaR-GNN with the strongest non-STaR-GNN method selected independently for each DMA, metric, and horizon. At 24 h, STaR-GNN was locally best for all four metrics in DMAs B–J; the four exceptions were confined to DMA A. The median margins over the strongest competitor across the ten DMAs were 16.8%, 18.2%, and 12.3% for MAE, MAPE, and RMSE, respectively, with a median NSE gain of 0.031. At 168 h, STaR-GNN remained best on all four metrics in DMAs B, C, D, F, H, and J and on the three error metrics in DMA I. The corresponding median margins remained positive—5.3%, 5.4%, and 4.3% for the three errors and 0.015 for NSE—but other methods performed better for all four metrics in DMAs A, E, and G and for NSE in DMA I. These cases are retained as negative margins in Fig. 3 rather than being removed through averaging.

The connected 24 h and 168 h margins further show that horizon extension affected the DMAs unevenly. The lead narrowed in several zones and changed sign in E and G, whereas DMA J showed larger week-ahead margins for all four metrics. This heterogeneity qualifies the system-level result: STaR-GNN's overall advantage was supported by a majority of DMAs and was not attributable to a single zone, but it did not imply universal local dominance. The available ten-DMA comparison cannot identify the demand or operational factors responsible for the exceptions; in application, the results instead support retaining DMA-specific performance monitoring when the forecast window is extended.

## 3.3 Component contributions and lead-time dependence

The factorial ablation in Table 2 separates the contributions of SAS-Norm and FA-DPR, while Fig. 4 tracks their day-wise effects throughout the 168 h horizon for all four metrics. The curves and confidence intervals are horizontally offset within each forecast day to distinguish the closely spaced estimates of DCRNN + SAS-Norm and the complete STaR-GNN without altering their temporal positions.

For 24 h forecasting, adding SAS-Norm to DCRNN reduced MAE, MAPE, and RMSE by 12.2%, 9.1%, and 10.4%, respectively. Adding FA-DPR alone reduced the same metrics by 5.7%, 12.1%, and 11.2%. The complete model produced larger reductions of 20.9%, 18.5%, and 19.2% and the highest NSE. Both components therefore contributed at the day-ahead horizon, and their effects were not fully redundant.

At 168 h, SAS-Norm accounted for most of the improvement in overall error magnitude. It reduced MAE, MAPE, and RMSE relative to DCRNN by 27.3%, 35.3%, and 34.1% and increased NSE from 0.940 to 0.974. FA-DPR alone reduced MAE by 16.2% and RMSE by 4.9% and increased NSE by 0.006, but its MAPE increased slightly from 3.248% to 3.278%. Its isolated contribution was therefore metric-dependent rather than uniformly beneficial. The complete STaR-GNN obtained the best 168 h MAPE, RMSE, and NSE (2.014%, 6.161, and 0.976, respectively).

The 168 h MAE requires a more qualified interpretation. DCRNN + SAS-Norm obtained 12.208, compared with 12.234 for STaR-GNN, a difference of only 0.026 (0.21% of the SAS-Norm value). The 95% moving-block confidence interval for the paired mean difference between the complete model and SAS-Norm was −0.129 to 0.177 and included zero. The two configurations should thus be regarded as statistically indistinguishable on this metric, whereas the complete model provided the more balanced result across MAPE, RMSE, and NSE.

The lead-time analysis further clarifies how these contributions developed over the forecast week. The MAE reduction of STaR-GNN relative to DCRNN increased from 13.4% on day 1 (95% CI: 10.6–16.1%) to 34.0% on day 7 (30.3–38.0%). SAS-Norm exhibited a similar increase, from 14.2% to 33.9%, confirming its central role in limiting week-ahead error growth. The MAE reduction from FA-DPR increased from 2.7% on day 1 to 18.5% on day 7, but its MAPE and RMSE deteriorated on some intermediate and late forecast days and their confidence intervals were wider. Taken together, the ablation supports complementary but unequal roles: SAS-Norm primarily stabilizes forecasts against shifts in demand level and scale, whereas FA-DPR provides forecast-position-specific information that improves selected long-lead outcomes. The present evidence does not justify claiming that FA-DPR alone improves every metric throughout the week.

## 3.4 Week-ahead demand dynamics

Aggregate metrics do not show when errors occur within the weekly demand trajectory. Figure 5 therefore links population-level error structure to a representative 168 h forecast. Figure 5a reports the mean absolute error in aggregate demand at each hour of the daily cycle after pooling all test windows and seven forecast days, with moving-block 95% confidence intervals. Figure 5b shows a representative forecast selected by a pre-specified median-error rule, and Fig. 5c locates the corresponding hourly absolute errors.

Averaged over the 24 within-day positions, the absolute error of STaR-GNN was 4.920 L s$^{-1}$, compared with 7.735 L s$^{-1}$ for DCRNN and 8.574 L s$^{-1}$ for STGCN, equivalent to reductions of 36.4% and 42.6%. STaR-GNN had the lowest mean error at 22 of the 24 hourly positions relative to DCRNN and at 23 positions relative to STGCN. Its advantage therefore covered most of the diurnal cycle instead of being concentrated in low-demand periods or a few hours. The one or two remaining positions also show why this result should not be interpreted as strict superiority at every hour.

The representative case was not selected for visual attractiveness. Applying the pre-specified rule that selects the common test window whose STaR-GNN total MAE is closest to the median identified index 70; its MAE was 12.182, compared with an overall median of 12.193. At the same window, total MAE was 15.517 for DCRNN and 14.653 for STGCN. All three models reproduced the principal daily periodicity (Fig. 5b), but STaR-GNN generally followed transitions in demand level and several local peaks and troughs more closely. The hourly errors in Fig. 5c nevertheless retain several spikes during rapid changes, indicating that short-lived transitions remain difficult to predict.

This example is intended to interpret, rather than replace, the full-period evaluation. The pooled diurnal analysis provides the population-level evidence; the median-error forecast illustrates how the improvement appears in a typical weekly trajectory; and the hourly-error panel identifies residual weaknesses that are averaged out by summary metrics.

## 3.5 Integrated discussion and implications

The experiments provide a coherent sequence of evidence. The overall comparison establishes that STaR-GNN improves both day-ahead and week-ahead forecasts across four complementary metrics, while the DMA-level distributions and local competitive margins show where that advantage persists and where it does not. The factorial ablation then attributes much of the long-horizon stabilization to SAS-Norm and identifies a smaller, metric- and lead-time-dependent contribution from FA-DPR. Finally, the week-ahead trajectory analysis connects the statistical gains to the diurnal structure of water demand. This convergence is more informative than any individual mean score because it tests accuracy, cross-DMA consistency, local heterogeneity, component contribution, and process-level behavior separately.

Previous work has shown that graph recurrent models can exploit inter-zone relationships in multi-DMA demand forecasting (Zanfei et al., 2022), while multi-scale architectures can improve forecasts by representing temporal patterns at different resolutions (Que et al., 2024). The present results are consistent with both observations but refine them in two respects. First, the comparison with DCRNN and STGCN shows that cross-DMA graph learning alone does not account for the observed gains. Second, the ablation and lead-time results suggest that explicitly separating changing demand state from within-day shape, then retrieving historical daily patterns according to forecast position, is particularly useful when the prediction window extends across several daily cycles. This is an evidence-based interpretation of the observed behavior, not a direct identification of the physical causes of demand variation.

The stronger long-horizon performance is relevant to urban water management. Day-ahead forecasts may inform short-term production and pumping schedules, whereas week-ahead forecasts may provide earlier information for storage planning, maintenance coordination, and resource allocation. The consistent reductions across most DMAs and hours indicate potential value for coordinated multi-zone forecasting. However, no downstream control or economic optimization was evaluated here; operational benefits should therefore be expressed as potential rather than demonstrated savings. The heterogeneity among DMAs and the exceptions in DMA G further indicate that deployment should retain zone-specific monitoring rather than rely solely on aggregate demand accuracy.

Four limitations define the scope of the findings. First, the evaluation concerns one real multi-DMA system, and transferability across cities, climates, and demand regimes remains to be tested. Second, the graph represents functional dependence inferred from historical demand rather than a complete hydraulic topology and is static across operating conditions. A dynamic graph or a hybrid functional–hydraulic representation may better capture seasonal and operational changes. Third, the recurrent and MSCMNet values in the broad comparison were taken from Que et al. (2024), whereas DCRNN, STGCN, and STaR-GNN were evaluated using the present pipeline. The cross-source comparison therefore establishes performance relative to existing benchmarks, while strict structural attribution relies on the graph-model and ablation comparisons conducted within the same evaluation pipeline. Finally, the present study evaluates deterministic point forecasts; predictive intervals and reliability under unusual demand events remain open questions.

Overall, STaR-GNN improved not only mean forecasting accuracy but also its consistency across horizons, most DMAs, and the diurnal cycle. The evidence indicates that explicit modelling of day-scale state changes and forecast-aligned daily patterns, combined with graph-based cross-DMA learning, is the principal reason that accuracy degrades more slowly over the week-ahead horizon.
